## 1. Physical background: Sauerbrey, Kanazawa–Gordon, bandwidth and dissipation

The gravimetric QCM rests on Sauerbrey's 1959 relation between frequency shift and areal mass [1] *(V)*. Operation in liquid was put on a quantitative footing by Kanazawa and Gordon, who showed that a semi-infinite Newtonian liquid shifts the resonance by Δf = −f0^{3/2} (ρ_L η_L / (π μ_q ρ_q))^{1/2} [2,3] *(V)*; both the 1985 *Analytical Chemistry* letter [2] and the companion *Analytica Chimica Acta* paper [3] state the (ρη)^{1/2} dependence. Martin, Granstaff and Frye extended this to simultaneous mass and liquid loading using a lumped-element (modified Butterworth–Van Dyke, BVD) model fitted to network-analyser data, and introduced the motional resistance as the liquid-loading observable [4] *(V for scope; details I)*.

For the bandwidth observable the modern convention follows Johannsmann: the half-bandwidth at half maximum Γ of the conductance peak, with the dissipation factor D = 2Γ/f_res = 1/Q, and the complex resonance frequency f̃ = f_res + iΓ [5,6,7] *(V)*. In a Newtonian liquid ΔΓ = −Δf (both ∝ n^{1/2}), so Δf/n^{1/2} and ΔΓ/n^{1/2} should collapse across overtones; Johannsmann's 2021 tutorial states explicitly that "square-root-n scaling is expected for the Newtonian liquid" and that data from different overtones overlap when divided by n^{1/2} [5] *(V from indexed extract)*. The same scaling was verified experimentally with an impedance analyser by Yoshimoto et al., who found the N-th overtone shift to be √N times the fundamental shift in a low-viscosity liquid [8] *(V from abstract)*.

Surface roughness perturbs this picture. Daikhin and Urbakh treated flow through a rough/porous interfacial layer with Brinkman's equation and showed that roughness adds a trapped-mass term (frequency only) and a dissipative term (both frequency and bandwidth), modifying the Kanazawa–Gordon term in proportion to h_r² [9,10] *(V from abstracts)*. In practice this means that liquid-phase validation of a new instrument should expect |Δf| and ΔΓ slightly larger than the ideal Kanazawa–Gordon value on rough electrodes, and that ΔΓ/(−Δf) ratios somewhat above 1 are not necessarily an instrument error [5,9] *(I)*. Kankare's "Sauerbrey equation in liquid medium" paper [11] *(I; DOI unverified)* and Johannsmann's small-load-approximation framework [6] *(V)* provide the general perturbation formalism used to interpret such deviations.

How authors define "error" against Kanazawa–Gordon varies. Olsson et al. used water–glycerol series and dissipation to separate viscous from mass loading [12] *(V from abstract)*. Fort et al. compared QCM-D and oscillator (QCM-R) front ends in Newtonian liquids of several viscosities against a commercial impedance analyser as reference, reporting accuracy and precision separately [13] *(V from abstract)*. Rodríguez-Pardo and co-workers characterised QCM sensors passively in highly viscous media and calibrated a phase-detector system with solutions of increasing viscosity, reporting sensitivity and resolution [14,15] *(V from abstracts; detail I)*. A consistent reporting pattern across these works is: (i) relative deviation of Δf and ΔΓ (or ΔD, or ΔR) from the theoretical value, per overtone; (ii) collapse of n^{-1/2}-scaled shifts; (iii) the ΔΓ/(−Δf) ratio as a check of Newtonian behaviour.

## 2. Interrogation methods for QCM in liquid

Several reviews organise the field: Arnau (2008) for liquid-phase interface circuits [16] *(V)*, Alassi, Benammar and Brett (2017) [17] *(V)*, Park and Choi (2020) [18] *(V)*, Lucklum and Eichelbaum's chapter on interface circuits [19] *(I)*, and the 2024 *Nature Reviews Methods Primers* primer by Johannsmann and Reviakine [7] *(V)*. They converge on four families:

1. **Oscillators** (Pierce/Miller/lever, with parallel-capacitance compensation and automatic gain control), with motional resistance or oscillation amplitude as a damping proxy. Rodríguez-Pardo and co-workers established design rules and noise limits for oscillators in liquid [20] *(I)*; the SRS QCM200 is a commercial example. Oscillators are cheap and fast but track a frequency that depends on the circuit (series resonance vs. zero-phase), and they struggle at high damping.

2. **Ring-down (QCM-D)**. Rodahl et al. (1995) introduced switching off the drive and fitting the exponentially decaying oscillation to extract f and D simultaneously [21] *(V for citation; method I)*. This is the basis of the Q-Sense/Biolin instruments [22] *(I)*. Addabbo et al. showed an FPGA reciprocal counter architecture that extracts f from short liquid-phase transients with sub-ppm resolution [23] *(V from abstract)*. Vendor literature argues ring-down is immune to stray capacitance and has lower drift than impedance analysis, while being more expensive [24] *(V from vendor page; claim not independently verified)*.

3. **Network/impedance analysis**. Beck, Pittermann and Weil (1988) are usually credited with the first impedance analysis of one-side-wetted crystals [25] *(U; details from memory)*. Schröder, Borngräber, Lucklum and Hauptmann (2001) built dedicated network-analysis interface electronics for QCM and extracted f from the conductance maximum [26] *(V)*. Commercial examples are the MicroVacuum/Gamry QCM-I, which determines "the resonant frequency and the bandwidth of the resonant conductance curve", FWHM being related to Q = 1/D [27] *(V from vendor document)*, the Gamry eQCM 10M, and the AWSensors platforms (which are based on Arnau's phase-shift concept) [16,28] *(I)*. Kasper et al. (2016) used a broadband 20 Hz–120 MHz impedance analyser to measure calibrated resistance and quantitative dissipation up to the 11th harmonic of 10 MHz crystals, resolving ΔR < 0.3 Ω (ΔD ≈ 0.01 µU) [29] *(V from abstract)*. Burda implemented a virtual impedance analyser with real-time BVD fitting, self-calibration and compensation for QCM in liquid [30,31] *(V from abstracts)*. Rodríguez-Pardo, Cao-Paz and Fariña applied passive (impedance) characterisation to highly viscous media [14] *(V)*.

4. **Phase-shift (fixed-frequency) methods**. Arnau, Montagut, García, Jiménez and co-workers drive the crystal at a fixed, very stable frequency and read the phase change of the sensor, which is proportional to mass loading in liquid (Δφ/Δm ≈ −1/m_L); the method was validated for in-liquid biosensing and liquid-property sensing [28,32] *(V from abstracts)*. Costas-Costas, Rodríguez-Pardo, Perrot et al. implemented and calibrated a phase-detector circuit as a QCM sensor for density–viscosity of fluids [15] *(V from abstract)*. These are single-frequency *phase* readers, not swept admittance analysers, and they do not reconstruct G(f).

The method under study belongs to family 3 (swept network analysis) but is implemented with the detector of family 4 (an analogue gain/phase chip). This hybrid is the crux of the novelty question (Section 7).

## 3. Resonance-parameter estimators and their bias

**Conductance maximum + half-height width.** The simplest estimator: f_res = argmax G(f), Γ from the two half-height crossings. It is what the QCM-I vendor document describes [27] *(V)* and what Schröder et al. used for f [26] *(V)*. It is unbiased only if the peak is symmetric and the baseline is flat; a baseline offset or a tilted/rotated peak biases both f and Γ, and the width estimate is sensitive to noise and to frequency-grid discretisation [33,34] *(V from abstracts of the microwave-resonator literature; QCM-specific statement I)*.

**Lorentzian fit.** Petersan and Anlage compared seven estimators of f and Q from complex transmission data (3 dB width, Lorentzian fit, phase-vs-frequency fit, resonance-curve area, Snortland, inverse mapping, etc.). They found the nonlinear least-squares fit to phase vs. frequency most accurate for SNR > 65, and the nonlinear Lorentzian fit best for noisier data; the 3 dB method is biased by background [33] *(V from abstract)*. Niedermayer et al. addressed significantly damped resonators (Q < 100, electromechanical resonators in viscous liquids), separating a second-order resonance from an unknown background spectrum [34] *(V from abstract)*. In QCM specifically, Johannsmann et al. and Leppin et al. extract f_res and Γ by fitting resonance curves to the admittance trace, "equivalent to impedance analysis" [5,35] *(V)*.

**Phase-shifted (rotated) Lorentzian.** The function used in the method under study is, in Johannsmann's notation [5] *(V from indexed extract of eq. 13 and surrounding text)*:

G_fit(f) = G_max Γ [ Γ cos φ /((f_res − f)² + Γ²) + (f_res − f) sin φ /((f_res − f)² + Γ²) ] + G_off,

with a companion B_fit. "The phase shift φ accounts for an asymmetry of the resonance curve. Imperfect calibration causes such an asymmetry." G_max, φ, G_off and B_off are treated as constant when converting admittance traces into resonance parameters [5]. Note the sign convention: with Δ = f_res − f the indexed extract shows "+ Δ sin φ", whereas the manuscript writes "(Γ cos φ − Δ sin φ)"; the two are equivalent under φ → −φ, but the manuscript should state its convention and cite eq. 13 accordingly *(V)*. The 2024 primer repeats that "a suitable fit function is the phase-shifted Lorentzian", described as a Lorentzian with "an additional set of three fit parameters (a phase, φ, and a complex offset)" [7] *(V from indexed extract)*. Leppin et al. (2020) used this fit on 32 simultaneously interrogated frequencies across four overtones at 10 ms time resolution [35] *(V)*.

Earliest use. The phase-shifted Lorentzian is the fit function of Johannsmann's impedance-analysis software (QTZ; the companion modelling package is QTM, freely downloadable) and appears in his group's work well before 2021. This session could confirm only that the 2021 tutorial [5], the 2020 fast-QCM paper [35], the 2015 monograph [36] *(I: book verified, chapter content not)* and the 2024 primer [7] use it, and that the group's 1999 *Macromol. Chem. Phys.* and 2004 *Langmuir* papers fitted resonance curves [37,38] *(U: whether the phase term was already present there could not be confirmed)*. Authors should cite [5] as the primary definition and [36] for the earlier description, and may say "introduced by Johannsmann and co-workers" without claiming a specific first-use year unless they check [37,38].

**The same rotation in microwave resonators.** The rotated Lorentzian is physically the same object as the "background phase" in microwave resonator metrology: a Lorentzian multiplied by a complex constant e^{iφ} (impedance mismatch / cable delay) appears as an asymmetric (Fano-like) line in the real part. Kajfez and Hwan's Q-circle method (1984) [39] *(I; DOI unverified)*, Petersan and Anlage's comparison [33], Khalil et al.'s diameter-correction method for asymmetric notch resonators [40] *(V from abstract)*, and Probst et al.'s algebraic circle fit with diameter correction [41] *(V)* all handle the rotation by fitting in the complex plane. A QCM reviewer who knows this literature would regard the phase-shifted Lorentzian as a special case of the complex-plane circle fit with a rotation parameter; the manuscript can gain credibility by making this link explicit and by noting that, unlike the full circle fit, the G-only phase-shifted fit uses one real channel and is therefore cheaper and robust to the AD8302 sign ambiguity in B.

**Admittance circle / BVD fitting in QCM.** Full BVD fitting of G and B (equivalently, fitting the admittance circle) yields R, L, C and C0 and is the standard "impedance QCM" analysis [4,30,31,42] *(V from abstracts; details I)*. It requires an accurate complex Y, hence a phase reference and calibration of stray elements; Burda's work stresses self-calibration and real-time compensation for exactly this reason [30,31] *(V)*. Niedermayer et al. and the NTU "spur-distorted spectra" work (not retrieved, *U*) indicate that spurious modes near the main resonance bias both Lorentzian and BVD fits unless a background term is included [34].

## 4. Gain/phase-detector (AD8302) approaches to impedance and to the QCM

The AD8302 (Analog Devices, 2001) outputs V_MAG = 0.9 V + 30 mV/dB × gain and V_PHS = 0.9 V − 10 mV/° × |Δφ| for |Δφ| in 0–180°, from LF to 2.7 GHz; its phase output is unsigned, folding at 0° and 180° [43,44] *(V from datasheet extracts; specific numbers to be checked against datasheet)*. This fold is the "phase-magnitude ambiguity" the manuscript must resolve.

**Impedance spectroscopy with the AD8302.** Yang et al. (2006) built a portable bioimpedance spectroscopy device around an AD8302 gain/phase detector, obtaining |V/I| in dB and the phase difference as voltages and converting to complex impedance [45] *(V from search extract; DOI unverified)*. Several later bioimpedance systems report accuracy enhancement of AD8302-based spectrometers at low frequency [46] *(I)*. Zompanti, Santonico and Pennazza (2026) proposed an AD8302-based "polar demodulator" for impedance spectroscopy from 100 Hz to >10 MHz with maximum modulus error 14.4 % and phase error 5.7° [47] *(V from abstract)*. A hobbyist 4.5–50 MHz impedance analyser built from an AD9851 DDS and an AD8302 is documented on the Analog Devices EngineerZone forum [48] *(V, grey literature)*. None of these address resonators or the sign ambiguity in the context of a resonance.

**AD8302 + QCM.** The only peer-reviewed combination located is Sakti (2019), who injected a sine wave into a QCM, measured gain and phase difference before/after the sensor with an AD8302, and compared the result with a Picoscope 5244B digital storage oscilloscope; the AD8302 "gave easy and direct results" that "can directly be processed using a microcontroller", and the author notes that QCM resonances at 5–20 MHz are within the device's range [49] *(V from abstract; whether complex Z was reconstructed from the divider and whether any liquid was tested could not be confirmed — U)*. No other journal paper pairing "AD8302" with "quartz crystal microbalance" was found in searches of general web, Semantic Scholar-indexed titles and Google-indexed PDFs; the AD8302 + magnetoelastic-resonance Arduino device [50] *(V from arXiv listing)* is the closest analogue outside QCM.

**openQCM Q-1 / NEXT.** The openQCM Q-1 is described by its maker as a "QCM-D based on a scalar network analyzer" in which an AD9851 DDS (120 MHz clock, DC–50 MHz) sweeps the crystal and an AD8302 measures the gain/loss through the sensor and the phase between actuation and response; the Teensy 4.0 (Cortex-M7, 600 MHz) digitises the detector outputs, and the Python host tracks frequency and dissipation "through the analysis of the resonance curve" [51,52] *(V from vendor pages and GitHub repository descriptions)*. openQCM NEXT adds simultaneous multi-overtone operation (n = 1, 3, 5, 7, 9) and Peltier temperature control; vendor specifications quote dissipation standard deviations in air of 0.1–0.3 ppm per overtone [52] *(V from vendor page)*. **No peer-reviewed paper describing the Q-1/NEXT electronics or validating them in liquid was found.** Third-party uses: Horst et al. (2022) built an open-source impedance QCM "based on the OpenQCM project" for < €200 with improved RF signal integrity, amplitude control and ~30 dB higher sensitivity, validated by Cu electrodeposition and aniline electropolymerisation (not by Kanazawa–Gordon) [53] *(V from abstract)*; Muñoz et al. (2023) built a low-cost QCM-D for biomedical use with active temperature control for < US$500 [54] *(V from abstract; whether the AD8302 front end was reused is U)*; a 2026 MDPI *Hardware* paper from the same Argentine group uses a NanoVNA-H as the acquisition device [55] *(V from DOI landing title; authors U)*; Zainuddin et al. (2018) verified a 3 MHz QCM array against a VNA using the original oscillator-based openQCM, finding 0.2 % frequency agreement [56] *(V)*. The 2016 J. Phys. Conf. Ser. "Open Source QCM with dissipation monitoring" is by Mista et al. (UNER, Argentina), not by the openQCM/Novaetech team [57] *(V)*. Important for the present manuscript: **the vendor documentation describes the existing Q-1/NEXT host software as operating on the scalar gain and phase curves (conductance-like peak and its width) — it does not, as far as could be verified, invert the voltage divider to complex Z/Y nor fit a phase-shifted Lorentzian** *(I; to be confirmed against the published Q-1 repository)*.

## 5. DDS-based and other low-cost / open-source QCM instruments

Beyond openQCM, the low-cost field includes: Arduino-oscillator arrays (Muckley et al., six QCMs for < US$1000, compared with a commercial QCM) [58] *(V)*; the Matusiak and Żak open-source thickness monitor [59] *(V from abstract)*; the NanoVNA-H-based QCM-D [55]; QCM-VNA systems using higher harmonics for microfluidic sensing (Koltuk et al., 2025) [60] *(V from record)*; Burda's PC-based virtual impedance analyser [30,31]; and the Siena group's FPGA QCM-D and front-end comparison [13,23]. The HardwareX/Hardware papers report bill-of-materials costs (< €200; < US$500); the Siena and Burda works do not quote cost. Of these, only Fort et al. [13] report a systematic liquid-phase metrological comparison (several viscosities, reference impedance analyser). DDS chips (AD9850/AD9851/AD9959) appear in openQCM Q-1/NEXT and in the Horst et al. derivative [51,53]; the "signal generator" in Sakti's work is not specified in the abstract [49].

Johannsmann's group's fast QCM [35] is the most advanced open-architecture impedance QCM found: it interrogates 32 frequencies over four overtones (15/25/35/45 MHz) simultaneously and fits phase-shifted Lorentzians at 10 ms resolution. Its hardware is not an AD8302 design *(I: architecture details not retrieved)*.

## 6. Real-time suitability and computational complexity

Argmax + half-height width is O(N) per sweep and trivially real time on a microcontroller or host. A four-parameter nonlinear phase-shifted-Lorentzian fit (G_max, f_res, Γ, φ, plus G_off) is O(k·N) for k Levenberg–Marquardt iterations; Leppin et al. ran such fits at 100 Hz on a PC [35] *(V)*. Full BVD fitting of G and B is comparably cheap once complex Y is available, but requires a phase reference and calibration [30,31]. The complex-plane circle fit of Probst et al. is algebraic and non-iterative and was specifically introduced for speed and robustness under noise [41] *(V)*. Ring-down requires fitting an exponentially decaying sinusoid per overtone and fast ADC capture [21,23].

## 7. Where the method under study sits

Combining the evidence above:

- Swept impedance/network analysis of QCM with f from the conductance maximum and Γ from the half-height width is classical [26,27,29].
- The phase-shifted Lorentzian is Johannsmann's established fit function [5,7,35,36]; the rotation is the same phenomenon as background phase in microwave resonator circle fits [33,39,40,41].
- The AD9851 + AD8302 + Teensy architecture is the published openQCM Q-1/NEXT hardware [51,52]; it has been reused by others [53,54] but not validated against Kanazawa–Gordon across overtones in a peer-reviewed paper.
- AD8302-based complex impedance reconstruction exists in bioimpedance spectroscopy [45,47] and in grey literature [48]; one QCM paper uses the AD8302 as a scalar gain/phase reader [49].
- No located work (i) inverts the R17 divider exactly to obtain Z_q and Y = G + jB from the AD8302 outputs, (ii) resolves the unsigned-phase fold using the resonance structure, (iii) fits the phase-shifted Lorentzian to the resulting G(f), and (iv) validates f_res and Γ in air, water and isopropanol on overtones 1–9 against Kanazawa–Gordon with the n^{1/2} law.

See `novelty_assessment.md` for the detailed statement.
