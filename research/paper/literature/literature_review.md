## 1. Physical background: Sauerbrey, Kanazawa–Gordon, bandwidth and dissipation

The gravimetric QCM rests on Sauerbrey's 1959 relation between frequency shift and areal mass [1] *(V)*. Operation in liquid was put on a quantitative footing by Kanazawa and Gordon, who showed that a semi-infinite Newtonian liquid shifts the resonance by Δf = −f0^{3/2} (ρ_L η_L / (π μ_q ρ_q))^{1/2} [2,3] *(V)*; both the 1985 *Analytical Chemistry* letter [2] and the companion *Analytica Chimica Acta* paper [3] state the (ρη)^{1/2} dependence; the original validation used aqueous glucose and ethanol solutions with an oscillator *(I)*. Martin, Granstaff and Frye extended this to simultaneous mass and liquid loading using a modified Butterworth–Van Dyke (BVD) model fitted to network-analyser data, and introduced the motional resistance as the liquid-loading observable [4] *(V for scope; details I, abstract not retrievable)*.

For the bandwidth observable the modern convention follows Johannsmann: the half-bandwidth at half maximum Γ of the conductance peak, the dissipation factor D = 2Γ/f_res = 1/Q, and the complex resonance frequency f̃ = f_res + iΓ [5,6,7] *(V)*. In a Newtonian liquid ΔΓ = −Δf and both scale as n^{1/2}; the 2021 tutorial gives the worked example −Δf = ΔΓ = 716 Hz/n^{1/2} for water on a 5 MHz crystal and states that data from different overtones overlap when divided by n^{1/2} [5] *(V, full text)*. Leppin et al. found power-law exponents of about 1/2 and ΔΓ = −Δf on n = 3–9 in water and a solvent mixture [35] *(V)*.

Experimental multi-overtone tests of the Kanazawa–Gordon relation by impedance analysis exist, all on bench or lab-built hardware. Yoshimoto et al. reported that the N-th overtone shift in a Newtonian liquid is √N times the fundamental shift, with the same trend in the BVD resistance [8] *(I: statement from an index record of the abstract; bibliographic data V)*. Bund and Schwitzgebel analysed 10 MHz resonators in water, methanol, ethanol, propanol, butanol and glycerol solutions, splitting Δf into rigid-mass and viscous terms [84] *(V abstract)*. Kankare, Loikas and Salomäki measured glycerol–water from 0 to 100 % with a swept DSB-SC/lock-in method and found the complex loading close to the shear acoustic impedance computed from ρη [79] *(V abstract)*. McHale et al. found the third harmonic of a 5 MHz crystal closest to viscometer values in ionic liquids, with a slope about 10 % below the Kanazawa–Gordon prediction [83] *(V abstract)*. Daridon et al. measured up to eight overtones in heptane and toluene, quoting about 5 % accuracy for viscosity from frequency and 2 % from bandwidth [80] *(I)*; Cassiède et al. showed that the fundamental deviates from the trend of the higher overtones because of finite-size and inharmonic-mode effects, and modelled both f and bandwidth with two interfacial parameters [81,82] *(I)*. Kowarsch et al. quantified compressional-wave contributions of about 30 Hz against a 700 Hz viscous shift for water at 5 MHz, stronger on the fundamental than on overtones [85] *(V)*. Hodges et al. report Newtonian sucrose solutions matching Kanazawa–Gordon [86] *(V abstract)*; Cao-Paz et al. found an oscillator sensitivity about 29 % above the theoretical value in acid solutions and set up an Allan-deviation resolution analysis [87] *(V)*. No paper tabulating per-overtone relative deviations of Δf and ΔΓ from Kanazawa–Gordon for water/alcohols/glycerol was found; the closest quantitative statements are those of [80,83,8].

Surface roughness perturbs this picture. Daikhin and Urbakh treated flow through a rough interfacial layer with Brinkman's equation and showed that roughness adds a trapped-mass term (frequency only) and a dissipative term (both), modifying the Kanazawa–Gordon term in proportion to h_r² [9,10] *(V from abstracts)*. Liquid-phase validation of a new instrument should therefore expect |Δf| and ΔΓ slightly larger than the ideal value on rough electrodes, and ΔΓ/(−Δf) somewhat above 1 is not necessarily an instrument error [5,9] *(I)*; Horst et al. invoked exactly this for an air-to-water shift larger than the expected 750 Hz [53] *(V)*. Kankare's "Sauerbrey equation in liquid medium" [11] *(V bibliographic)* and Johannsmann's small-load approximation [6] *(V)* provide the perturbation formalism.

How authors define "error" against Kanazawa–Gordon varies. Olsson et al. used water–glycerol series and dissipation to separate viscous from mass loading [12] *(V from abstract)*. Fort et al. compared ring-down (QCM-D) and oscillator (QCM-R) front ends in water and glucose solutions against a Wayne Kerr 6500B impedance analyser, validating against a modified BVD with Newtonian-liquid impedance rather than against Kanazawa–Gordon directly; QCM-D was more accurate, QCM-R more precise [13] *(V, full text)*; the same group's 2021 oscillator paper used water/glycerol [101] *(V)*. Rodríguez-Pardo and co-workers characterised sensors passively in highly viscous media and calibrated a phase-detector system with solutions of increasing viscosity [14,15] *(V from abstracts)*. Liao et al. compared oscillator frequency shift with impedance analysis against a rotational viscometer (max relative error ±5.2 % vs ±1.8 %) [98] *(V)*. A consistent reporting pattern is: (i) relative deviation of Δf and ΔΓ (or ΔD, ΔR) per overtone; (ii) collapse of n^{−1/2}-scaled shifts; (iii) the ΔΓ/(−Δf) ratio as a Newtonian check.

## 2. Interrogation methods for QCM in liquid

Reviews: Arnau (2008) [16] *(V)*, Alassi, Benammar and Brett (2017) [17] *(V)*, Park and Choi (2020) [18] *(V)*, Lucklum and Eichelbaum's chapter [19] *(V bibliographic)*, the 2024 *Nature Reviews Methods Primers* primer [7] *(V bibliographic; body paywalled)*, and Skládal's 2024 piezoelectric-biosensor review, which is the only peer-reviewed text found that states the openQCM AD9851 + AD8302 architecture (from vendor material) [62] *(V)*. Four families:

1. **Oscillators** (Pierce/Miller/lever, with parallel-capacitance compensation and automatic gain control). Rodríguez-Pardo and co-workers established design rules for oscillators in liquid [20] *(V bibliographic)*; the SRS QCM200 is a commercial example. Cheap and fast, but they track a circuit-dependent frequency and struggle at high damping.

2. **Ring-down (QCM-D)**. Rodahl et al. (1995) introduced switching off the drive and fitting the decaying oscillation to extract f and D [21] *(V bibliographic; content I)*; Rodahl et al. 1997 extended it to biomolecular adsorption and cell adhesion [88] *(V bibliographic)*; this is the basis of the Q-Sense/Biolin instruments [22] *(I)*. Addabbo et al. showed an FPGA reciprocal counter (Artix-7, 1-bit undersampling or analogue mixing, no ADC) extracting f from liquid-phase transients at 120–240 ppb, tested in water and glucose 9–16 % w/w; dissipation is not estimated there [23] *(V, full text)*. Vendor literature argues ring-down is immune to stray capacitance and has lower drift than impedance analysis [24] *(V from vendor page; not independently verified)*; Johannsmann's view is that the interrogation methods "do not, actually, differ much from each other in precision" [5] *(V)*.

3. **Network/impedance analysis**. Beck, Pittermann and Weil (1988) are credited with the first impedance analysis of one-side-wetted crystals [25] *(V bibliographic; cited for this by [5])*. Schröder et al. (2001) built dedicated network-analysis interface electronics and extracted f from the conductance maximum [26] *(V)*. Commercial examples: the MicroVacuum/Gamry QCM-I, which fits the conductance curves to obtain the frequency of the maximum and the FWHM (fit model unspecified) [27] *(V)*, the Gamry eQCM 10M, and the AWSensors platforms [16,28] *(I)*. Kasper et al. used a broadband impedance analyser with equivalent-circuit fitting up to the 7th harmonic in liquid (11th/23rd in air), resolving ΔR < 0.3 Ω and ΔD ≈ 0.01 µU; liquids were PBS and NaCl, with no water/glycerol series [29] *(V, full text)*. Burda implemented a virtual impedance analyser on an Analog Discovery 2 with a reference-resistor half-bridge, both channels sampled, BVD by direct extremum methods and by Levenberg–Marquardt, self-calibration and compensation, in water, glycerol–water 15–50 % and glycerin [30,31] *(V, full text)*; a 2023 paper treats the spurious modes [100] *(V)*. Rodríguez-Pardo, Cao-Paz and Fariña applied passive characterisation to highly viscous media [14] *(V)*.

4. **Phase-shift (fixed-frequency) methods**. Arnau, Montagut, García, Jiménez and co-workers drive the crystal at a fixed, stable frequency and read the phase change, proportional to mass loading in liquid; validated for biosensing and liquid-property sensing [28,32] *(V from abstracts)*. Costas-Costas et al. implemented and calibrated a phase-detector circuit for density–viscosity (chip not named in the abstract) [15] *(V from abstract)*. The same group used a DDS + AD8302 at fixed frequency for a Love-wave immunosensor in liquid [64] *(V)*, and Li and Bhethanabotla a DDS + gain/phase detector for a SAW system with a sweep mode compared against a VNA [65] *(V)*. These are single-frequency phase readers; they do not reconstruct G(f).

The method under study belongs to family 3 but is implemented with the detector of family 4. This hybrid is the crux of the novelty question (Section 7).

## 3. Resonance-parameter estimators and their bias

**Conductance maximum + half-height width.** f_res = argmax G(f), Γ from the half-height crossings: the QCM-I note [27] *(V)* and Schröder et al. [26] *(V)*. It is unbiased only for a symmetric peak on a flat baseline; a baseline offset or a rotated peak biases f and Γ, and the width is sensitive to noise and grid discretisation. Robinson and Clegg showed that half-power-bandwidth interpolation is inaccurate with noisy or coarsely sampled data and that a quadratic fit of 1/P needs a quarter of the points for the same Q accuracy [94] *(V)*. QCM-specific refinements: Kang et al. average the resonant frequency from the medians of the conductance peak at several heights, gaining about eightfold in SNR over the equivalent-circuit value [95] *(V abstract)*; Na Songkhla and Nakamoto refine the conductance maximum by a windowed fit with Savitzky–Golay smoothing in glycerol–water [96] *(V abstract)*; Arnau's review recommends a plain Lorentzian fit over direct reading of the discrete peak for heavy loading [16] *(V)*. Guha et al. derive f and D analytically from each impedance point of a fixed-frequency drive [97] *(V abstract)*.

**Lorentzian and complex-plane fits.** Petersan and Anlage compared seven estimators on complex S21 data: the nonlinear phase-vs-frequency fit is best for SNR > 65, the Lorentzian fit (with background, slope and a real "skew" term) for noisier data; circle fits rotate and translate the resonance circle to the origin [33] *(V, full text)*. Niedermayer et al. treated significantly damped resonators (Q < 100, viscous liquids) by separating the resonance from an unknown background spectrum [34] *(V; author list confirmed)*; Voglhuber-Brunnmaier et al. showed the method reaches the Cramér–Rao bound at low noise [91] *(V)*; Reindl et al. give Cramér–Rao bounds for sampled damped phasors and reject parabolic peak interpolation [92] *(V)*; Sader et al. give closed-form bias and variance of least-squares resonance fits under multiplicative noise [93] *(V)*. In QCM, Johannsmann et al. and Leppin et al. obtain f_res and Γ by fitting resonance curves to the admittance trace [5,35] *(V)*.

**Phase-shifted (rotated) Lorentzian.** The fit function used in the method under study is, in Johannsmann's notation [5, eq. 13] *(V, full text)*:

G_fit(f) = G_max Γ [ Γ cos φ + (f_res − f) sin φ ] / ((f_res − f)² + Γ²) + G_off,  B_fit(f) = G_max Γ [ Γ sin φ + (f_res − f) cos φ ] / ((f_res − f)² + Γ²) + B_off,

"The phase shift φ accounts for an asymmetry of the resonance curve. Imperfect calibration causes such an asymmetry. The asymmetry can be small, but it rarely vanishes." The compact form Y(f) = e^{iφ}·iΓG_max/(f_res − f + iΓ) + G_off + iB_off, six real parameters, is eq. 3 of Leppin et al. 2020, with the remark that the ideal Lorentzian "does not cover the parallel capacitance C0 and it does not catch imperfect calibration" [35] *(V, full text)*. Sign convention: with Δ = f_res − f eq. 13 has "+ Δ sin φ"; the manuscript's "(Γ cos φ − Δ sin φ)" is the same function with φ → −φ and should say so. The 2024 primer's glossary defines the phase-shifted Lorentzian as a fit function "that accounts for calibration artefacts by allowing for asymmetry and offsets" [7] *(I)*; the group's Analyst 2021 SI reportedly uses it "because of imperfect calibration" [103] *(I)*.

Earliest use. Neither [35] nor [5] cites an origin for the function. The 2015 monograph lists G_max, G_off, B_off and the phase φ as fit parameters of its eq. 4.5.30 (Section 4.5, equivalent electrical circuits), so the same parameter set was in print by 2014 [36] *(V for the chapter glossary; the section itself was not readable)*; a Google Books search for the phrase "phase-shifted Lorentzian" in the book returned nothing, so the name may be later than the function *(I)*. Whether the group's 1992 *Phys. Rev. B*, 1997 *Faraday Discuss.*, 1999 *Macromol. Chem. Phys.*, 2004 *Langmuir*, 2007 chapter or 2008 *PCCP* papers already include the phase term could not be confirmed or excluded; their bibliographic records are verified but the texts were inaccessible [114,115,37,38,116,6] *(U for content)*. A Europe PMC full-text search for "phase-shifted Lorentzian" finds no QCM paper before 2020. The group's software is QTM, now PyQTM, a viscoelastic-model fitter, not a resonance-curve fitter [102] *(V)*; the "QTZ" software named in version 1 of this review does not exist (the string occurs only as image file names on the group's pages) — corrected. Authors should cite [5] as the definition, [35] and [36] as prior use, and say "introduced by Johannsmann and co-workers" without a first-use year.

Independent use of the same function. The Arnau/AWSensors group holds a 2025 US patent (priority July 2020) that fits G and B simultaneously to a six-parameter Lorentzian with "an angle that takes into account the small slopes that may appear in the resonance curve", by Nelder–Mead, citing no source for the form [112] *(V)*. The Shull group's open-source rheoQCM fits G and B jointly with a phase-shifted Lorentzian (φ bounded to ±π/2, "zerophase" option) in code dating from at least 2019, without citation [107] *(V, source read)*; Brass and Shull 2008 used a plain Lorentzian on the conductance peak [113] *(V)*. The 2026 Kırımlı preprints fit a plain Lorentzian plus offset to G, citing Arnau 2008 [61] *(V)*; the NanoVNA QCM-D code of [109] does the same *(V, grey)*. The rotated form is therefore established in at least three independent QCM groups.

**The same rotation in microwave resonators.** The rotated Lorentzian is the "background phase" of microwave resonator metrology. Kajfez and Hwan adapted Ginzton's impedance method to the network analyser [39] *(V bibliographic; whether an explicit angle is defined there, U)* and Kajfez later used linear fractional curve fitting of the complex reflection coefficient [118] *(V)*; Leong and Mazierska removed rotation and offset of the measured circle for high-Q dielectric resonators [119] *(V abstract)*; Petersan and Anlage include a real skew term in |S21| and a rotation in the circle fit [33] *(V)*; Gao's 2008 thesis puts the factor e^{iφ_0} inside the line shape (eq. E.1) and also uses a skewed Lorentzian for |t21|² [117] *(V)*; Khalil et al. identify φ as "the rotation angle of the resonance circle around the off-resonance point", derive it from impedance mismatch and introduce the diameter-correction method [40] *(V)*; Probst et al. implement it algebraically [41] *(V)*; McRae et al. summarise the φ rotation method as the first to account for mismatch [120] *(V)*. A reviewer from that community will read the G-only phase-shifted Lorentzian as the single-quadrature special case of the rotated circle fit; the manuscript should say so and note that the G-only fit is chosen because the AD8302 sign ambiguity makes B less reliable.

**Admittance circle / BVD fitting in QCM.** Full BVD fitting of G and B yields R, L, C and C0 and is the standard "impedance QCM" analysis [4,30,31,42] *(V bibliographic; details V for [30,31])*. It needs an accurate complex Y, hence a phase reference and stray-element calibration; Burda's self-calibration and compensation address exactly this [30,31] *(V)*. Spurious modes near the main resonance bias both Lorentzian and BVD fits unless modelled: Yoon, Cho and Kanazawa separate a spur from the main resonance [99] *(V abstract)*; Burda 2023 characterises two spurious modes in air, water and glycerol [100] *(V)*; Johannsmann notes that overlapping anharmonic sidebands in liquid make fitted f and Γ "vary unsystematically" [5] *(V)*.

## 4. Gain/phase-detector (AD8302) approaches to impedance and to the QCM

The AD8302 (Analog Devices, 2001) outputs V_MAG = 0.9 V + 30 mV/dB × gain and V_PHS = 0.9 V − 10 mV/° × |Δφ| for |Δφ| in 0–180°, 0–1.8 V, from LF to 2.7 GHz; its phase output is unsigned, folding at 0° and 180°, and is least accurate near those limits [43,44] *(V from datasheet mirrors and the ADI product page; the Analog Dialogue article by Cowles and Gilbert states that for 360° operation "the user must determine independently which quadrant the phase is in", I)*. This fold is the ambiguity the manuscript must resolve.

**Resolving the fold.** The RF literature resolves it in hardware: McDermott and Ireland switch the detector reference between in-phase and quadrature signals and take atan(I/Q) [71] *(V)*; Wetterlin inserts a switchable −90° reference shift and advises staying ≥ 20° from 0° and 180°, where errors reach 8° [72] *(V, grey)*; Mlynek et al. and Grossmann et al. add a second AD8302 fed with a 90°-shifted reference [70,69] *(V)*; Pokorný et al. shift the operating point of a resonant sensor to about 90° because of the nonlinearity near zero phase [73] *(V abstract)*. Hobby VNA software (miniVNA/Zplots) infers the sign of the reactance from the trend of |Z| *(I, grey)*. No published resonator work resolves the fold from the resonance structure itself.

**Impedance spectroscopy with the AD8302.** Yang et al. (2006) built a portable bioimpedance spectrometer on the magnitude-ratio/phase-difference method with three-reference calibration, 20 kHz–1 MHz, 0.014 % and 0.136° mean error on RC networks [45] *(V abstract; chip attributed to it by later authors)*; Mohamadou et al. (2018) addressed its low-frequency accuracy [46] *(V bibliographic)*; Ciarrocchi, Zompanti, Santonico and Pennazza (2026) place the unknown impedance in the feedback of a dynamic-gain inverting amplifier with a reference-resistor bank and reach 100 Hz–10 MHz with maximum errors of 14.4 % and 5.7°, noting the largest inaccuracies near 0° and ±180° but not resolving the sign [47] *(V, full text; first author Ciarrocchi, omitted in version 1)*; Ben Atitallah et al. compare the AD8302/shunt-resistor method (|Z| = ratio × R_shunt) with other embedded methods [74] *(V)*; Nogales et al. apply it to Li-ion battery EIS with 52.3 Ω input matching and an unexplained "±" in the phase law [75] *(V)*; Kassanos et al. and Ausín et al. describe the discrete AD8302 approach before integrating it [76,77] *(V)*; Santoso et al. is cited by others as an AD8302 system [78] *(I)*. All convert the dB ratio and phase to polar Z; none treats a resonator.

**AD8302 + QCM.** The only peer-reviewed combination located is Sakti, Akbar, Kamasi and Naba (2019): an AD9910 DDS drives a 10 MHz crystal in series with a 50 Ω 0.1 % reference resistor; the AD8302 outputs are read by a PIC18F45K50 ADC and compared with a PicoScope 5244B. |Z| is computed from the magnitude ratio and the phase is plotted separately — no G + jB, no divider inversion to R and X, no f or Γ estimate — and only air was tested; the oscilloscope was judged closer to the BVD model [49] *(V, full text; version 1 had the author list incomplete)*. No other journal paper pairing "AD8302" with "quartz crystal microbalance" was found (Europe PMC full-text, Crossref, OpenAlex, web). Other resonators read with the AD8302: Love-wave and SAW sensors at fixed frequency in liquid [64,65] *(V)*, an LC pressure resonator swept by a DDS with the resonance taken from the phase extremum [66] *(V)*, a phononic-crystal liquid sensor [67] *(V)*, an RF-MEMS IR sensor [68] *(V)*, and the magnetoelastic device of Silva, Cunha and Mendes (AD9833 DDS, ATmega328P, pickup coil vs generator reference; magnitude and phase plotted separately, Q from the magnitude FWHM, tested in water, fold not resolved) [50] *(V, full text)*.

**openQCM Q-1 / NEXT.** The vendor describes the Q-1 as a "QCM-D based on a scalar network analyzer": an AD9851 DDS sweeps the crystal, an AD8302 measures gain and phase through the sensor, a Teensy 4.0 digitises the outputs and the Python host tracks frequency and dissipation from the resonance curve [51,52] *(V)*. openQCM NEXT adds n = 1–9 (sequential, about 1.4 s per overtone) and Peltier control; vendor specifications quote, in liquid, frequency σ of 2.8–2.0 Hz and dissipation σ of 3.9–34 ppm for n = 1–9, and 0.1–0.3 ppm dissipation σ in air [52] *(V vendor pages)*. The published Q-1 v3.0 host software (GitHub openQCM/openQCM_Q-1) was read for this review: it subtracts a calibration baseline polynomial from the raw AD8302 magnitude, applies Savitzky–Golay smoothing and spline interpolation, takes the argmax, walks to 0.707 × peak on each side for the bandwidth, and sets Q = f/bandwidth, D = 1/Q; the phase trace is acquired and plotted but not used by the estimator; there is no divider inversion and no Lorentzian fit *(V, source)*. A December 2025 vendor post explains that the NEXT uses a per-overtone calibrated cut-off ΔA_n instead of 0.707 and calls the result "instrumental dissipation" [110] *(V)*. **No peer-reviewed paper describing the Q-1/NEXT electronics or validating them against Kanazawa–Gordon was found**; Skládal's review restates the vendor description [62] *(V)*, and Miranda-Martínez et al. used a Q-1 as the reference instrument against a rotational viscometer in water, acetone, isopropanol, glycerin/sugar/salt solutions and artificial body fluids, finding it closer to the viscometer than their own prototype but saturating in highly viscous liquids [63] *(V)*. Application papers using the NEXT in liquid exist (e.g. [111], n = 3 only) but none validates the instrument. Third-party derivatives: Horst et al. (2022) kept the AD9851 and Teensy 4.0 but replaced the AD8302 by a single-ended AD8310 logarithmic amplifier (no phase), added an AD8007 buffer and a 7th-order Chebyshev filter, extracted f and D from a cubic spline of the magnitude, and validated in water (air-to-water shift larger than the expected 750 Hz, attributed to roughness) and by Cu electrodeposition and aniline electropolymerisation, for a BOM of €176 [53] *(V, full text; version 1 wrongly listed gain/phase)*; Muñoz et al. (2023) used a commercial DG8SAQ VNWA (S11 → Y11), f = argmax G, D = FWHM/f, in air, water and PEG solutions, fundamental only [54] *(V, full text; resolves the version 1 (U))*; the same group's 2026 *Hardware* paper uses a NanoVNA-H and a Pi Pico PID, the third harmonic, PEG 2–10 %, for < US$500 [55] *(V, full text)*; Zainuddin et al. verified a 3 MHz array against a VNA with the original oscillator openQCM [56] *(V)*. The 2016 "Open Source QCM with dissipation monitoring" is a ring-down module by Mista et al. (UNER), not by the openQCM team [57] *(V)*. Important for the manuscript: **the published Q-1/NEXT software operates on the scalar magnitude curve; it does not invert the divider to complex Z/Y and does not fit a Lorentzian** *(V, source)*.

[13], on a lab setup without BOM. DDS chips appear in openQCM Q-1/NEXT, Horst et al. [53], Sakti et al. (AD9910) [49] and Silva et al. (AD9833) [50].

Johannsmann's group's fast QCM [35] is the most advanced open-architecture impedance QCM found: a 32-tone comb with lock-in detection over four overtones fitting phase-shifted Lorentzians at 100 Hz; not an AD8302 design *(V)*.

## 6. Real-time suitability and computational complexity

Argmax + half-height width is O(N) per sweep and trivially real time on a microcontroller or host; the Q-1 host does it at the sweep rate *(V, source)*. A phase-shifted-Lorentzian fit (G_max, f_res, Γ, φ, G_off) is O(k·N) for k Levenberg–Marquardt iterations; Leppin et al. ran six-parameter fits at 100 Hz on a PC [35] *(V)*; the AWSensors patent uses Nelder–Mead [112] *(V)*. Burda rejects nonlinear fitting for real-time use and extracts BVD parameters by closed-form extremum methods at 192 points/s [31] *(V)*. The complex-plane circle fit of Probst et al. is algebraic [41] *(V)*; Guha et al. obtain f and D analytically per point [97] *(V)*. Ring-down requires fitting a decaying sinusoid per overtone and fast capture [21,23].

## 7. Where the method under study sits

- Swept impedance analysis with f from the conductance maximum and Γ from the half-height width is classical [26,27,29]; its bias under noise and coarse grids is documented [94,95,33,34].
- The phase-shifted Lorentzian is Johannsmann's established fit function [5,7,35,36], used independently by AWSensors [112] and the Shull group [107]; the rotation is the background phase of microwave circle fits [33,40,41,117,120].
- The AD9851 + AD8302 + Teensy architecture is the published openQCM Q-1/NEXT [51,52,62]; its host software uses the magnitude only *(V, source)*; it has been reused with a different detector [53] and used as a reference instrument against a viscometer [63], but not validated against Kanazawa–Gordon across overtones in a peer-reviewed paper.
- AD8302 polar-impedance reconstruction through a known resistor is prior art in bioimpedance/EIS [45,46,47,74,75]; the one AD8302 QCM paper stops at |Z| and phase in air [49]; the fold is resolved in hardware in the RF literature [69,70,71,72].
- Multi-overtone Kanazawa–Gordon tests exist on bench analysers [8,79,80,81,83,84,35,5]; none on a DDS/gain-phase-detector instrument.
- No located work (i) inverts the divider exactly to Z_q and Y = G + jB from the AD8302 outputs on a QCM, (ii) resolves the unsigned-phase fold using the resonance structure, (iii) fits the phase-shifted Lorentzian to G(f) obtained this way and compares it with argmax/half-height on the same sweeps, and (iv) validates f_res and Γ in water, isopropanol and glucose solutions on overtones 1–9 and two instruments against Kanazawa–Gordon with the n^{1/2} law.
